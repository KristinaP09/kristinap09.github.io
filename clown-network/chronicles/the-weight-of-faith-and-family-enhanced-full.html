The Weight of Faith and Family

A Personal Essay

Sometimes, I think the words “friend,” “family,” and “community” packed up and escaped me — probably out of self-respect. Who can blame them? Around here, those words are just decorative — embroidered on banners, preached from pulpits, and forgotten in practice. I’ve even tried to resurrect them, redefine them, but they keep deleting themselves from my vocabulary like tired software that doesn’t want to function in a corrupted system.

Yesterday, I decided to re-run my little experiment in social decay. A harmless test, really — to see if humanity had updated since last time. Spoiler alert: it hasn’t. My mother — the woman I defend through hurricanes of contradiction — told a church member who bought an umbrella, “asa tor sombuh” (“hope someone gets better”). I wasn’t sick, but the irony stung. In this small, sanctified circle of “faith,” every smile hides a sting, and every blessing doubles as a critique. Passive-aggression wrapped in holy language — that’s the local dialect.

Church life? A weekly masquerade of holiness. Everyone parades their piety like it’s Fashion Week for the Righteous. Sunday service isn’t worship; it’s brand management. People recite scripture like spell codes, pretend to care, and then go back to being professional hypocrites for the rest of the week. If hypocrisy were a tradable commodity, we’d be billionaires. They call it fellowship. I call it spiritual theater.

I used to think being decent would shield me — no debts, no scandals, no chaos. Just quiet perseverance. But here, decency doesn’t make you respectable — it just makes you a more convenient servant. The good ones are easy to exploit. The crooked ones thrive. My siblings mastered this: cheer in public, sabotage in private. Their love has conditions, hidden fees, and expiration dates. I stopped confiding in them. Privacy is my last form of freedom, and they treat it like contraband.

So yes, call me anak durhaka — the ungrateful one. I’ll take that crown gladly. The first valuable lesson I ever learned? Leave your family before they drain your soul dry. And trust me, I’m trying. But walking away from that kind of emotional conditioning isn’t easy. Since childhood, I’ve been trained to believe that responsibility equals love.

And the worst part? It’s worked. It’s made me unprepared for everything — forever guilty, forever responsible, never free. Sometimes I even pray, “God, please give her long life, because I’m not ready to carry what she’ll leave behind.”

And the thing is — this isn’t just a family problem. It’s a national performance. In Indonesia, patriarchy is preserved like an ancient relic — dusty, outdated, but guarded like treasure.

Because here’s the truth — culture isn’t a shield for incompetence. Tradition isn’t an excuse for abuse. And submission isn’t respect.

Maybe one day, I’ll know what freedom feels like — not the loud, decorative kind, but the quiet one that comes when you finally stop mistaking pain for love.

After going through all that — the lies, the hypocrisy, the polished cruelty — I’ve lost whatever faith I had left. Religion, friends, family, community… they’re all just different brands of the same illusion. I don’t believe in any of them anymore.
